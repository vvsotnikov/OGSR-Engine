# Per-stalker scheduler cadence and combat

The scheduler kept every stalker observed alive throughout each measured window updating, including at +400. However, +400 produced measurable deadline slippage and budget exhaustion. This is evidence against complete starvation in these short runs, not proof of unchanged responsiveness or an unlimited population ceiling.

## Method

Same Bar seed, whole-map policy, stationary actor, hardware/settings and dispersed placement as `STRESS-2026-10-03.md`. Each process uses private appdata, waits 15 seconds, spawns its batch, warms up 15 seconds, measures 30 seconds, and quits normally. No Tracy collector was connected. One run per population; simulation outcomes remain stochastic.

Separate diagnostic package `bin_cadence`, ReleaseTracyProfiler, executable SHA-256 `4183452A7536EE072B3B7F073E860E946D5632D6F36258BD6C52102EC9379B94`, based on `54e8d91f4aeb853f80dc598cb2a3c706189d35da`. Matching binaries, PDB, exact source patch, headers and build log are retained locally. Instrumentation writes buffered CSVs and scans the scheduler queue: its cost is included, not independently measured. The hooks do not change scheduler deadlines or budgets.

The census enumerates client `CAI_Stalker` objects once per second, including those with no callbacks. The analyzed window spans the first through last measurement census (about 29 seconds, 30 samples). Its persistent cohort contains IDs observed alive at every census. This is sampled survival, not proof of continuous presence between samples. These are human stalker objects, unlike the earlier server-creature counters that include monsters/groups.

At each stalker scheduled callback, a matching scheduler context supplies its requested interval and due time. Actual callback-entry time comes from the same global timer as the scheduler, rather than the frame timestamp. Lateness is actual entry minus due time; actual intervals are differences between consecutive entries for that ID. Context mismatches and unexpected realtime stalkers fail analysis. Per-NPC output includes zero-update NPCs and boundary-inclusive maximum silent gaps; all-alive dispatch statistics also include NPCs that die during the window. Percentiles use nearest rank.

The existing scheduler deliberately slows distant NPCs: `CObject::shedule_Scale()` uses camera distance/200; stalker limits 100/250 feed the scheduler's effective maximum `(1000+250)/2 = 625 ms`. Therefore an approximately 625 ms interval does not by itself indicate overload. Due times are based on frame time, so requested intervals, actual intervals and measured lateness are distinct quantities.

No persistent-cohort NPC had zero updates. At +400, 35 of 27,385 callbacks were more than 50 ms late; none exceeded 100 ms. The worst individual NPC's p95 lateness was 38 ms. Normal combat was not disabled in the dispersed sweep: +400 logged 757 shots from 66 spawned NPCs. It is not an idle-only population benchmark or a standardized battle.

## Controlled combat setup

A separate 20-fighter scenario selects accessible navigation positions around a fixed center 40–80 metres from the actor, within 14 metres of the center, with 3-metre spacing and alternating opposing sides. Fighters receive AK-74s and ammunition. After warmup, explicit relations make the actor/native stalkers friendly to the participants and the two participant teams hostile to one another. Normal scripts, movement and mortality remain enabled; identical initial placement does not imply identical engagement positions or battle outcomes.

The first run recorded 70 shots from 19 fighters and 1,754 callbacks with a participant selected as an enemy. Living participants changed from 20 to 19. Living participant dispatch lateness was p99 6 ms, maximum 7 ms; the scheduler never exhausted its budget. First observed enemy selection ranged from 310 to 3,021 ms after hostility for 19 participants. This includes perception, behavior and callback sampling and must not be described as pure scheduler or combat reaction latency.

A second fresh-process run again recorded 70 shots from 19 fighters, with 1,657 participant-enemy callbacks and 20 to 19 living participants. All 20 eventually had a participant selected as an enemy. Living fighter dispatch lateness was p99 6 ms, maximum 8 ms; no scheduler budget stops or remaining overdue work occurred. Mean frame time was 4.18 ms, p99 6.21 ms. First observed enemy selection had median 883 ms but maximum 23,765 ms, versus 3,021 ms in the first run. That large variation despite prompt scheduler dispatch shows why fixed initial placement alone is insufficient for a standardized reaction-time benchmark. Future battle scaling should record positions/line of sight at hostility and distinguish perception/decision latency from scheduling.

Both runs verify actual engagement in a small encounter. They do not establish large-battle or dense visible-crowd performance.
